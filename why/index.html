Why bother

The club is ours
in every way
but one.

Supporters built it, fill it, fund it and outlast every owner it has ever had. They just do not own any of it.

Nobody needs convincing that something is off. A supporter born in Walton has less say in Liverpool Football Club than an investor who has never been to Anfield and never will. That is not an accident or a scandal — it is simply how the ownership of English football is arranged, and it has been arranged that way for a long time.

What has changed is that it is now possible for a very large number of people to put a small amount of money behind the same thing, at the same time, without any of them having to trust an organiser with it. That is new. It is the only reason this is worth attempting now rather than in 1992.

It is not a new idea, just a new method

Fan ownership is ordinary almost everywhere except England.

Germany

Most clubs operate under the 50+1 rule, where members hold a majority of voting rights. Bayern Munich has hundreds of thousands of members. Borussia Dortmund is publicly listed and still bound by it.

Spain

Barcelona and Real Madrid are owned by their members — tens of thousands of socios who elect the president. Not a minority stake. The whole club.

England, when fans had no other choice

AFC Wimbledon and FC United of Manchester were both built from nothing by supporters who had lost their clubs. Both are member-owned, one member one vote, and both are still here.

The difference is that those were either protected by national rules or started from scratch after a disaster. Nobody has worked out how supporters buy into an existing Premier League club that nobody is giving away. That is the actual problem.

Be honest about what a small stake gets you

Not control. Let us say that first and plainly, because a movement that oversells itself deserves what it gets.

Liverpool is worth billions. A realistic fan holding — one or two per cent — would not give supporters a veto, a board seat as of right, or the power to overrule an owner. Anyone telling you otherwise is selling something.

What it does give you is harder to put in a headline and worth more than it sounds:

A shareholder is in the room

Not a consultation exercise or a fans' forum that meets twice a year. A registered holder of shares, with the rights that carry, and a published mandate it is obliged to follow.

A number that cannot be dismissed

"Fans are unhappy" is an opinion. "Forty thousand supporters have put real money behind this and here is the proof" is a fact, and it changes what a board can comfortably ignore.

A permanent holding that does not get bored

Owners come and go on a ten-year cycle. A supporters' society does not sell, does not take dividends, and is still there for the owner after next.

A foundation rather than a finished building

One per cent is not the goal, it is the proof. The hardest part of any movement is the first stone. Nobody buys a majority of anything as their opening move.

Why money in, glory out

Every pound that leaves a football club to pay its owners is a pound that did not sign a player, fix a stand, or hold a ticket price down. Supporters already know this, which is why so many of them resent the arrangement regardless of results.

So this cannot be a way to make money from the club without also becoming part of the problem. Hence the rule that nothing is ever paid out to anybody, written into the declaration and, if we get far enough, written into the society's constitution in a form no vote can remove.

It sounds like a sacrifice. It is actually the only thing that makes the proposition coherent. A supporter who owns part of their club does not want an income from it. They want the club to be stronger, and they want a say in how it is run.

Why it will probably fail

It would be strange to ask for your money and not say this part.

No owner may ever sell to us. Football's regulators may decide a movement shaped like this cannot hold a stake. We may never raise enough to matter. Bitcoin may halve while people's money is committed. Any of those ends it.

What makes it worth attempting anyway is the shape of the downside. If this fails, every supporter's money returns to them on the date they chose, because it never went anywhere else. There is no pot to lose, no company to go bust, no administrator to appoint. The worst case is that a few thousand people tied up some money for a year, got it back, and proved it could be done — which is more than anyone has managed so far.

The risks are set out properly, and less kindly, on the risks page. Read it before committing anything.

What we are asking

Right now, nothing but your name.

Signing the declaration costs nothing, moves no money, and commits you to no payment ever. It says you want this to exist. That is the only thing being asked for today, and it is the only thing that can turn a document into a movement.
