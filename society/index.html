The plan for what comes after the fund

Who would
actually
own it?

The fund can raise money. It cannot own anything. A stake in a football club has to be held by something with a legal existence, run by rules its members can enforce. This page is the plan for that, and it is only a plan: nothing described here has been formed.

Two bodies, two jobs

The Kop Fund and the society are deliberately different things. Keeping them apart is part of the design, not a loose end.

The society comes late on purpose. Forming a legal body early would tie us to obligations before there is anything for it to hold. The hard part was never filling in a form. It is that nobody has to sell us anything.

What kind of body

We expect it to be a community benefit society, a legal form that supporters' trusts in Britain commonly use. Three things about it matter to us:

One member, one vote

Its rules give every member a single vote, whatever they put in. A wealthier supporter is not a more important one.

An asset lock

Its rules must keep what it owns for the benefit of its community. Nothing can be paid out to members as profit. That is the legal form of "money in, glory out", and it is the reason the rule can be written in a way no later vote removes.

A register and a regulator

A society keeps a register of its members and is registered with a regulator in Britain. That is why joining it means giving a name, which the fund never asks for.

We are not lawyers. This is the shape we are aiming for, and a solicitor decides whether it works.

How you would join

A nominal share, around £1

It buys the vote and nothing else. It earns no return, and it is not the money you commit through the fund. Put your name to it and you get a vote.

A fee each season, not once

Membership lapses if you stop paying, so the vote belongs to people still paying attention. The fee is meant to cover the society's own running costs, such as keeping its register. It is kept apart from committed bitcoin and does not go towards a purchase.

One membership per person, checked

A low price does not stop one person casting fifty votes. The check does. Exactly how it is done without the fund learning anything is not worked out.

These amounts are the current intention, not a promise, and they are in pounds because the society would be a British body. Whether and how supporters outside Britain can join is a question for the solicitor.

Why joining and committing are separate

It would be tidier to say you must have locked bitcoin to be a member. We decided against it, for a reason that cannot be engineered around. A lock expires on a date, so the only way for the society to confirm someone is still locked would be to connect their name to their bitcoin address. That would end the anonymity the fund exists to keep.

The split also gives each side a check on the other:

Contributors can fund, but not direct

No money is spent without their commitments. Nobody steers the club through the fund.

Members can direct, but not spend

The society votes the club's shares. It cannot reach into anyone's wallet.

Contributors can always walk away

If the society went wrong, nobody has to back its next purchase, and every lock still goes home on its date.

It also costs something, and we would rather say so. The two groups will not be the same people: someone can lock a great deal and never join, or join and never lock. And a society that is cheap to join could in principle be taken over by a bloc of people who put nothing into the stake. Named members, one membership each and the asset lock (a captured society still cannot pay anything out) make that harder. They do not make it impossible, and it is on the list for the solicitor.

A tension with the declaration

The declaration says nobody has to say who they are and that no database of supporters will be built. It also says members get a say. Put next to this plan, that needs spelling out.

The fund will never ask for your name and will never keep a database of supporters. The society has to keep a register of its own members, and a say in directing the club comes through that register, so using it means giving your name to the society, never to the fund. Anyone who has signed the declaration can choose to join. Nobody has to.

Whether that squares with the declaration's exact wording is a question for the solicitor. If it does not, we will publish a second version of the declaration with the reason and ask signers whether they want to sign it. That is the process the declaration itself sets out.
