The questions people actually ask

How it works

No jargon, no hand-waving. If any answer here does not satisfy you, that is a problem with the design rather than with you.

Where does my money actually go?

Nowhere. That is not a figure of speech — it is the whole design.

You keep your bitcoin in a wallet only you control, exactly where it already is. There is no account to pay into, no address to send to, and no pot anywhere. What you do instead is lock your own coins, which means putting a rule on them that stops you spending them before a date you pick. The money is still yours and still in your hands. It is just not available to you for a while, and anybody in the world can see that it is not.

This is why there is nothing for an organiser to run off with. Not because we are trustworthy, but because we never have it.

Why would I lock my own money up?

Because otherwise the number means nothing.

If this movement counted bitcoin that supporters merely owned, the total would be a measure of how much crypto its sympathisers happen to hold — which no club owner, journalist or solicitor would take seriously for a second, and nor should they. A locked coin is different. It is money somebody has deliberately put beyond their own reach, and that is a fact anyone can check against the blockchain.

You pick the length: one year, three or five. Longer is a louder statement, and the register shows who has gone deepest.

What happens when the lock runs out?

Your coins become spendable by you again, and nothing else happens.

Nobody has to agree to it. No form, no notice period, no asking permission. On the date you chose, your own signature is enough to move your money and nobody else is involved in any way. If this movement has collapsed, if the people running the register have vanished, if everyone else has lost interest — your money still comes home on the day you picked.

It also stops counting towards the total that day, whether you have touched it or not. A total that included money people could spend tomorrow would not be a total of commitments.

If nobody holds the money, how does anything ever get bought?

Your locked coins can leave in exactly two directions, and no others.

Home to you — your signature alone, on or after your date.

Into a purchase — your signature plus a second one, at any time.

When there is something to buy, each supporter commits their own locked money to that specific purchase. Your commitment is your payment. There is no moment where the money sits in a fund waiting, which means there is no moment where it could be taken, misdirected or lost.

And because a commitment is your own signature on your own payment, a vote can never move anybody else's money. A thousand people voting yes cannot touch a penny belonging to someone who voted no.

Who holds that second signature, and what can they do with it?

Less than you would think, and that is deliberate. Right now it is one supporter, named publicly, and here is the exact extent of it:

They cannot take your money. Your own signature is needed alongside theirs, every time, for every coin.

They cannot keep your money. The route by which your lock comes home does not involve them at all. They could lose the key, change their mind or disappear, and your coins would still return to you on your date.

They can refuse to sign. That is the whole of their power. It would cost the movement an opportunity. It cannot cost you a penny.

Which is worth saying plainly for a reason that is not modesty: that key is worthless to a thief. It cannot move money on its own, so stealing it, or threatening its holder for it, would get somebody nothing at all.

What do I get out of it?

A say. Never money.

If this ever owns a stake in Liverpool, that stake will be held by a supporters' society whose own rules force it to vote the club's shares the way its members vote. Not consult them. Not take their views into account. Vote as they voted, on the public record, so anyone can check it did.

There are two kinds of decision and they do not use the same rule. Spending money is weighted by what each member has committed, because it is their money and nobody should be able to out-vote the people putting it up. Directing the club is one member, one vote, because a wealthy supporter is not more of a supporter.

Why bitcoin? Why not just a bank account?

Because a bank account has somebody's name on it.

Every pooled fund needs a person or company to hold it, and that is the single point everything else fails at: they can be pressured, they can make mistakes, they can be sued, they can help themselves. Bitcoin is the only money that lets a hundred thousand strangers commit to the same thing while each of them keeps their own, provably, with no trusted middle.

It also means anyone anywhere can join without paperwork — which matters when the people who care most about an English football club are scattered across the world.

The cost is real and we are not hiding it: bitcoin's price moves, sometimes brutally, and while your money is locked you cannot do anything about that. It is the first thing on the risks page.

Do I have to use your app or your website?

No, and that is a rule rather than a courtesy.

This movement is a set of published documents, not a piece of software. Anyone can write their own app from them, or use an ordinary bitcoin wallet and no app at all, and the register has to treat them exactly the same. Anyone can also run their own copy of the register — the page reads one public file and checks it in your own browser, so if your total ever disagrees with ours, ours is wrong.

A signature cannot be forged and needs nobody's permission to pass on. So if we ever refused to publish yours, you could hand it to anybody else who would. There is no gate for us to keep.

What if the person running this gets hit by a bus?

Your money is unaffected — it comes home on its date regardless, with nobody's cooperation required.

The movement itself is a harder question, and the honest answer is that it is one person at the moment, which is a weakness rather than a feature. The mitigation is that everything is written down and published: the declaration, the specifications, the code, and the record of every change. Somebody else could pick it up without needing anything that only exists in one person's head.

Things we got wrong and changed

Published because a design that has never been corrected has usually just never been checked.

We were going to pool the money

The first version had one shared treasury with elaborate rules to stop anyone draining it. Testing found seven ways to break it, two of which lost everything. The rules were not the problem — pooling was. Removing it deleted every one of those attacks at once.

We then removed locks entirely

For a while the plan was to count supporters' live balances instead, on the grounds that a lock was unnecessary friction. That was wrong: a balance proves somebody owns bitcoin, not that they have committed any. Locks came back — but as the thing that counts, not as a barrier to joining.

We nearly promised never to change the declaration

Dropped, because the first solicitor to read it may well say something must be worded differently, and a document that loudly declares itself permanent and then changes looks worse than one that never claimed it. Instead each version is dated and fingerprinted, and signatures belong to the version they were made over.

Every change is in the public commit history, and the specifications are published in full. Nothing about how this works is a private arrangement. The documents are here.

Still not settled

Open questions, listed because finding them on your own later would be worse.
